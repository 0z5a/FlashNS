\begin{abstract}
Training a physics-informed network on a high-order objective needs
coordinate derivatives of the network and the complete parameter gradient
of a loss built from them. FlashNS propagates only the derivative state that
a fixed residual consumer reads, and transposes every local dependency of
that state into the parameter gradient. For the gradient-enhanced
two-dimensional Navier--Stokes objective, an eight-channel state (GL8:
value, gradient, Hessian and gradient of the Laplacian) replaces the
ten-channel third-order jet, while its parameter transpose requires the
fourth activation derivative. On an NVIDIA H20 in FP64, all 72 planned
solves pass unchanged accuracy gates and an independent scalar-AD check;
GL8 reaches the gates 1.66$\times$ faster than a compiled full-jet control
and 1.73--3.37$\times$ faster than matched adaptations of collapsed Taylor
mode, explicit differentiation and exact STDE. On the transient
three-dimensional Ethier--Steinman benchmark, a 17-channel contract passes
all gates in 18 of 18 H100 solves and is 3.60$\times$ and 6.96$\times$
faster than internal full spatial-jet and naive baselines. A 36-channel
curl-potential contract gives 1.32$\times$ objective-gradient throughput on
H100; with a larger L-BFGS budget its training passes the loss gate and most
physical gates, but stratified pressure still fails.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Training a physics-informed neural network (PINN) requires derivatives of a
network output with respect to coordinates and derivatives of the resulting
loss with respect to every network parameter. These are different consumers.
For gradient-enhanced incompressible Navier--Stokes training, the residual
uses second coordinate derivatives and its gradient uses third derivatives.
A fast residual evaluation does not establish a fast training step, and a
fast training step does not establish a shorter solve to a prescribed error.

Taylor-mode and forward-propagated derivative methods already address the
cost of nested coordinate automatic differentiation
(AD)~\cite{griewank,taylorjax,fwdlap}. Residual-specific partial jets,
collapsed Taylor propagation and stochastic Taylor contractions are
especially relevant to this problem~\cite{cao,cmtad,stdepp}. We build on
that setting. Our question is whether a concrete operator contraction, its
complete parameter transpose and a practical bounded working-state schedule
can shorten an unchanged third-order training solve beyond a compiled
full-jet implementation and public derivative methods.

For this objective, independent third partials are not needed: the diffusion
consumer asks for the gradient of the Laplacian. We retain the scalar value,
coordinate gradient, symmetric Hessian and gradient of the Laplacian. In two
dimensions this is an eight-channel state, GL8, instead of the ten channels
of a complete third-order state, Q10. Closure through an activation includes
products between lower derivatives. Its parameter transpose additionally
depends on the fourth activation derivative. Omitting that dependence would
change the parameter gradient even if some forward residuals agreed.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Consumer contracts.} We state closed derivative states for two
fixed consumers: GL8 for the two-dimensional gradient-enhanced
Navier--Stokes residual, C17 for a transient three-dimensional
velocity--pressure residual and its spatial gradient, and C36 for a
transient three-dimensional curl-potential momentum residual and its
gradient. Each contract is closed
under affine layers and smooth pointwise activations, and the runtime
rejects observables the contract cannot answer.
\item \emph{Complete parameter transposes.} We derive and implement the
reverse rules for these states, including the fourth activation derivative
that the forward state never stores, the transpose of packed symmetric
storage, and a transpose of the stable coefficient program that is actually
executed in saturated regions.
\item \emph{Execution.} Library GEMMs, explicit pointwise derivative kernels
and CUDA Graph replay of the complete loss/gradient program are combined
with value-only boundary rows and a chunked mode that accumulates the
globally weighted gradient in a bounded workspace.
\item \emph{A solve-level comparison protocol.} Every method trains from
identical data and initial parameter bytes under one optimizer program and
unchanged accuracy gates, with an independent scalar-AD final check, pinned
public sources and every attempt retained. Ratios are reported only for
jointly accepted pairs. We apply it to eight baselines in two dimensions
and to two internal baselines on a three-dimensional benchmark, and report
the C36 extension, including its failed acceptance.
\end{enumerate}

The paper does not claim the first residual-aware derivative method, a
globally minimal state, a pure representation effect separated from
execution, or superiority across PDEs, precisions or GPU families. The
two-dimensional H20 campaign, the C36 study (H100 and CPU) and the
Ethier--Steinman solves (H100) use different models, data, seeds, gates and
hardware; their timings are never pooled. Table~\ref{tab:terms} defines the names used throughout.

\section{Related work}
Physics-informed networks put PDE constraints into a training
objective~\cite{pinn}; gradient-enhanced training also penalizes residual
derivatives~\cite{gpinn}. NSFnets apply PINNs to the incompressible
Navier--Stokes equations in velocity--pressure and vorticity--velocity
forms, including Kovasznay flow and the three-dimensional
Beltrami (Ethier--Steinman) flow~\cite{nsfnets}.
Our contribution concerns execution of an unchanged derivative-intensive
objective rather than a new loss or architecture.

Univariate Taylor propagation and its higher-order reverse sweeps are
classical algorithmic differentiation~\cite{griewank}. Taylor-mode AD in
JAX avoids the redundant work of nesting first-order AD~\cite{taylorjax}.
Forward Laplacian propagates value, gradient and Laplacian forward through
a network to evaluate a second-order operator for neural variational Monte
Carlo, with large speedups over nested differentiation~\cite{fwdlap}. GL8
applies the same forward-propagation principle to a third-order consumer.
It must additionally retain the full symmetric Hessian, because nonlinear
convection gradients read it, and it supplies a hand-written complete
parameter transpose.

Cao et al.~\cite{cao} already exploit residual-dependent partial jets
and specialized layer rules, checking residuals and parameter gradients.
FlashNS therefore does not claim priority for propagating only
PDE-relevant derivative information. Dangel et al.~\cite{cmtad} collapse
derivative sums within Taylor-mode computation for linear differential
operators, closely related to retaining contractions instead of a full
tensor. STDE and its extension~\cite{stde,stdepp} use high-order Taylor
directions and contractions to avoid materializing large derivative
tensors; the JMLR code link currently points to the public STDE
repository~\cite{stdecode}. We evaluate the public implementations of these
three methods, adapted to the same objective, rather than comparing only
against our own compiled full-jet backend.

The three-dimensional model learns a vector potential and takes its curl,
so the velocity is divergence-free by construction. Neural Conservation
Laws parameterize divergence-free fields with differential forms and prove
universality of that construction~\cite{ncl}. We use the classical
vector-potential form only as a model choice; our contribution there is
the derivative contract that its curl and transient momentum gradient
require.

\section{Contracted derivative execution}
\subsection{A fixed scientific objective}
We study steady incompressible flow with constant viscosity $\nu$ and physical
Cartesian coordinates $x=(x_1,x_2)$. A neural network predicts $(u,v,p)$.
Its residual is
\begin{align}
r_u&=u u_{x_1}+v u_{x_2}+p_{x_1}-\nu\Delta u,\\
r_v&=u v_{x_1}+v v_{x_2}+p_{x_2}-\nu\Delta v,\\
r_d&=u_{x_1}+v_{x_2}.
\end{align}
Following the gradient-enhanced objective~\cite{gpinn}, we retain both the
residual and its coordinate gradient:
\begin{equation}
L=\tfrac12\operatorname{mean}_{\Omega}
\left(\|r\|_2^2+0.2\|\nabla r\|_F^2\right)
+5\left(\operatorname{mean}_{\partial\Omega}
\|(u,v)-(u_*,v_*)\|_2^2+
\operatorname{mean}_{x_1=1}(p-p_*)^2\right).
\end{equation}
This objective needs third coordinate derivatives. Removing the gradient
penalty would change the scientific problem. All implementations use the
existing point weights and validation definitions; in particular, the
boundary component RMS is not the training vector-square mean.

\subsection{A closed representation}
For a scalar intermediate $z$, define
\begin{equation}
S(z)=(z,g,H,t),\qquad g=\nabla z,\quad
H=\nabla^2 z,\quad t=\nabla\Delta z.
\end{equation}
Only independent symmetric Hessian entries are stored. In two dimensions
the raw, unnormalized state is
\begin{equation}
(z,z_{x_1},z_{x_2},z_{x_1x_1},z_{x_1x_2},z_{x_2x_2},
 z_{x_1x_1x_1}+z_{x_1x_2x_2},
 z_{x_1x_1x_2}+z_{x_2x_2x_2}).
\end{equation}
This is GL8, compared with the ten channels of Q10. The raw-to-Taylor
projection includes the appropriate factorials; GL8 channels must not be
read as Taylor coefficients.

For a pointwise activation $y=\phi(z)$, let $a_k=\phi^{(k)}(z)$. Direct
application of the chain rule gives
\begin{align}
g_y&=a_1g,\\
H_y&=a_1H+a_2gg^\top,\\
t_y&=a_1t+a_2\left(g\,\operatorname{tr}H+2Hg\right)
          +a_3g\|g\|_2^2.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{align}
An affine layer applies the same weight matrix to each state channel,
with the bias added only to the value channel. Consequently, affine
layers and smooth pointwise activations form a closed execution rule for
$S$. The state supplies every quantity in $r$ and $\nabla r$: convection
gradients need the Hessian, while diffusion gradients need $t$.
Figure~\ref{fig:method} summarizes the forward closure and its transpose.

Closure is sufficient for this consumer, not a claim of global
minimality or a reconstruction of all third derivatives. For example,
$z=x_1^3-3x_1x_2^2$ has $S(z)=0$ at the origin but
$z_{x_1x_1x_1}=6$. A request for an independent third partial cannot be
answered from GL8. The runtime rejects unsupported coordinate identities
or contractions instead of returning such an observable.

\subsection{Complete parameter adjoints}
Reducing the forward state alone is insufficient for training. FlashNS
transposes the same local closed rules, including their dependence on
the primal value. To make that dependence explicit, define
$Q=g\operatorname{tr}H+2Hg$ and $c=g^\top g$. The differential of the
third contracted component is
\begin{align}
\delta t_y={}&a_1\delta t+
a_2\left(\delta g\,\operatorname{tr}H+
g\,\operatorname{tr}\delta H+2\delta H g+2H\delta g\right)\nonumber\\
&+a_3\left(c\,\delta g+2g(g^\top\delta g)\right)
+\left(a_2t+a_3Q+a_4gc\right)\delta z.
\label{eq:differential}
\end{align}
Thus the local reverse rule requires $a_4$, although the forward
coordinate state stops at order three. The value, gradient and Hessian
differentials are transposed together with~\eqref{eq:differential}.
For the packed symmetric Hessian, off-diagonal entries use the transpose
of symmetric unpacking: the two matrix contributions are added, not
treated as two independent stored channels. Appendix~\ref{app:transpose}
gives the complete local transpose.

If $X$ is a layer input state and $\bar Z$ its preactivation adjoint,
the complete parameter updates are
\begin{equation}
\bar W=\sum_{n,q}\bar Z_{nq}X_{nq}^{\top},
\qquad \bar b=\sum_n\bar Z_{n,0},\qquad
\bar X_{nq}=W^\top\bar Z_{nq}.
\end{equation}
The implementation uses flattened point/channel matrix multiplications
for these reductions. A fixed polynomial residual program seeds all nine
residual and residual-gradient terms. It is a specific, verified consumer
rule rather than a general symbolic residual compiler.

In exact arithmetic, projection of a complete coordinate jet commutes
with the closed affine and activation rules. Composing with a loss that
depends only on that projection therefore preserves its parameter
derivative by the ordinary chain rule. Our numerical checks test both
the projected forward quantities and the entire flattened parameter
gradient; forward agreement alone would not establish this property.

\subsection{Stable activation and bounded working state}
The production kernels reuse the existing stable $\tanh$ convention.
In particular, the first derivative is evaluated with a stable
exponential tail instead of relying exclusively on subtraction from a
rounded saturated value. Higher derivatives and their reverse rules use
the same primal convention. Affine work remains in library GEMMs;
pointwise activation and residual transposes use explicit CUDA kernels.
CUDA Graph replay reads the current parameter storage rather than a
snapshot, which is checked by consecutive in-place updates.

Dirichlet and outlet consumers that need only values use a separate
value-only path. At 2,048 interior and 512 boundary points this reduces
logical point/channel rows from 25,600 for full Q10 rows everywhere to
20,992 for interior-only Q10 and 16,896 for interior-only GL8; these are
workload counts, not measured speedups. The chunked implementation
processes interior points in blocks of 512 and accumulates the complete
parameter gradient using the original global weights. It never normalizes
each chunk separately. Its working-state bound is proportional to chunk
size, while fixed data, parameters and optimizer storage remain outside
that bound (Appendix~\ref{app:chunk}). Floating reduction order can alter
line-search trajectories; every backend must still satisfy the same
independent final criteria.

The measured GL8 production scope is two-dimensional, constant-viscosity,
pointwise affine and $\tanh$ networks with first parameter VJPs. The current
contract does not support a learned contraction, nonlinear coordinate
changes, sample-coupled layers, nonlocal losses, HVPs or double backward.

\section{Two-dimensional experimental protocol}
\subsection{Problem, data and acceptance}
The analytic Kovasznay solution is
\begin{equation}
u_*=1-e^{\lambda x_1}\cos(2\pi x_2),\quad
v_*=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}e^{\lambda x_1}\sin(2\pi x_2),\quad
p_*=\frac{1-e^{2\lambda x_1}}{2},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{1}{2\nu}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\nu^2}+4\pi^2}.
\end{equation}
We set $\nu=0.07$ on $[-0.5,1]\times[-0.5,1.5]$. The network has widths
$2$--$64$--$64$--$3$, two $\tanh$ hidden layers, a linear head and 4,547
parameters. Training uses 2,048 interior points and 128 points on each of
four edges. The 128 pressure-outlet points share the last edge's coordinates;
there are 512 boundary coordinates, not 640. We retain the original globally
normalized interior, velocity and outlet weights.

The fixed stop-check set contains 8,192 interior points and 257 points on
each boundary edge. A separate scalar nested-AD interior set verifies every
candidate final solution. Both validations must meet all six limits in
Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}, with finite values. PDE RMS combines the three
residuals by the original sum-of-squares convention. Boundary velocity RMS
averages velocity components, whereas the training loss uses their vector
square. We preserve these definitions rather than reconstructing them from
metric names. Five data/weight SHA256 values are identical across every
matched run; initialization affects parameters only.

\subsection{Optimizer, stopping and finite attempts}
Every formal arm uses the same optimizer and stopping program. Adam permits
4,000 updates with learning rate $10^{-3}$, halved every 1,000 updates,
$\beta=(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$. Stop checks occur before training
and every 100 Adam updates. Strong-Wolfe L-BFGS then permits at most 500
blocks, each with 20 iterations, nominal 25 evaluations, history size 50,
learning rate 1, gradient tolerance $10^{-12}$ and change tolerance
$10^{-15}$. Stop checks follow each block. Line searches may exceed a
nominal evaluation limit; reported closure counts are actual counts and
include the Adam evaluations.

The two-dimensional formal seeds are 862101--862106. Development seeds
361901 and 361902 and older regression seeds are excluded from the formal
comparisons. No seed, threshold or optimizer budget is changed after an
observed result. Every attempt is retained. Nonconvergence consumes its
measured cost and counts against the attempted arm; an unsupported
execution variant does not turn into a successful baseline through
substitution. Paired time ratios are computed only when both members pass
and are accompanied by all outcomes.

The Q10/GL8/chunk campaign balances the six permutations of three arms.
The subsequent public comparison has six paper-group arms; three sampled
permutations and their reverses balance their order over the same six seeds.
NVIDIA baselines use separately admitted windows, so their timings are not
interleaved with paper-group controls. Each window runs one GPU process at
a time; Appendix~\ref{app:records} gives the device and window records.

\subsection{Baselines and execution modes}
\textbf{Q10 compact} propagates the complete two-dimensional third-order
Taylor state at interior points and values at boundaries. It uses the
existing explicit parameter transpose, full-graph Torch compilation and
CUDA Graph replay. It is a strong internal representation control, not the
public collapsed Taylor package.

\textbf{Cao ED} calls the pinned author's \texttt{ed\_u\_jet} without changing
that function. Its value, first derivatives and diagonal second derivatives
assemble the Navier--Stokes residual. Coordinate AD of that residual adds
the original gradient-enhancement term; parameter AD supplies the complete
gradient. This is a matched-objective adaptation of the public implementation.

\textbf{Collapsed Taylor} calls the public \texttt{jet(collapsed=True)}
transform with exact coordinate directions. The collapsed second-order
output supplies the Laplacian, and residual coordinate AD supplies the
additional third-order consumer. Parameter AD is included. We test both the
default complete loss/gradient program and its traced, compiled variant.

\textbf{STDE exact} calls the public sparse-STDE operator
\texttt{hess\_diag} with dimension two, no randomized Jacobian and
\texttt{rand\_batch\_size=0}.
The two coordinate directions are exhausted, so this experiment uses no
stochastic derivative estimator. JAX forward residual differentiation and
parameter backward preserve the same loss. JAX runs on the GPU in FP64;
CPU fallback is rejected. DLPack exchange, copies and synchronization with
the common Torch optimizer are included in replay and solve wall time.
This experiment does not evaluate every STDE++ mixed-partial construction
or its intended high-dimensional stochastic setting.

\textbf{Torch AD} evaluates nested coordinate AD and complete parameter
backward on an ordinary dense MLP. \textbf{PhysicsNeMo}~\cite{physicsnemo} uses the public
\texttt{PhysicsInformer} with symbolic Navier--Stokes equations and its
autodiff gradient method, followed by residual-gradient AD and parameter
backward. It is the full residual path, not just a standalone gradient
component.

\textbf{cuDNN}~\cite{cudnn} uses an algebraically equivalent $1\times1$ convolution MLP
with coordinate and parameter AD. A cuDNN operator must appear in the actual
profiler trace; a fallback is retained as a failed qualification rather than
called a cuDNN result. \textbf{cuEquivariance polynomial}~\cite{cueq} reuses the inherited
FP64 Q10 segmented-polynomial forward and explicit parameter VJP with the
native \texttt{uniform\_1d} backend. That implementation retains its
unmodified all-points Q10 policy, including boundaries. Scalar-irreps
\texttt{Linear} fused-tensor-product variants are separate diagnostics and
cannot replace the polynomial formal arm. Actual native operators are
required in the profiler.

For Torch-based public adapters and exact STDE, high-order derivatives run
only at the 2,048 interior points and values only at 512 boundary points,
matching the Q10/GL8 controls' point policy. Public adapters use
\texttt{torch.tanh}, while production FlashNS preserves its stable tail
convention. Mathematically they represent the same activation; numerical
full-gradient equivalence is checked rather than assumed. Pinned source
commits are listed in Table~\ref{tab:sources}.

\subsection{Qualification, caches and timing scopes}
Qualification evaluates the complete loss and all 4,547 parameter gradients
against the full-jet reference after each of three consecutive in-place
updates. The unchanged numerical tolerance is absolute $2\times10^{-12}$
and relative $2\times10^{-11}$. It also checks actual native execution where
required. Passing default/compiled variants are selected by the smallest
development median complete-gradient wall time; the selection map and
report hashes are frozen before formal seeds. No failed variant is used.

Qualification warms task-owned Torch, native-extension, Triton and JAX
caches. Each formal attempt is a fresh process with new parameters,
optimizer state and graphs. Persistent JAX compilation caching is explicitly
enabled, with compile threshold zero and entry threshold $-1$; cache
settings and hit/miss logs are retained. The resulting solve experiment is
a warm-dependency execution comparison. Initial qualification/build cost
is reported separately and is not silently amortized over the six seeds.

Complete-gradient qualification uses five groups of 30 replays after a
two-second warm-up and reports the median group wall time. The earlier
representation/memory study instead uses five independently ordered
processes per shape/arm, each with seven groups of 100 replays; these are
separate experiments. Replay timing includes full loss, every parameter
gradient, chunking and interoperation costs, but excludes optimizer, stop
validation and setup.

Optimization wall time includes training, optimizer work, line searches,
synchronization, stop checks and independent final acceptance. Setup plus
optimization additionally includes per-run engine preparation. It excludes
interpreter startup, common data creation and teardown; full child-process
wall time includes those costs. We report paired geometric means of time
ratios, not ratios of unpaired medians. Small-sample ranges are descriptive,
not a general-PDE confidence guarantee.

All matched experiments use FP64 with TF32 disabled on an NVIDIA H20 (SM90,
97,871 MiB), driver 580.76.05, CUDA toolkit 13.0.88, Torch 2.12.1+cu130,
Triton 3.7.1, JAX 0.11.2 and the native cuEquivariance ops package 0.12.0.

\subsection{What the reported solve times measure}
Across all 54 accepted public solves the largest final $v$ relative error
is 0.0199--0.0200 for every method, at its limit of 0.02, while the other
five criteria retain margin (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). The relative
$L_2$ error of $v$ is therefore the binding criterion in this problem: a
reported solve time is, in practice, the time to the first stop check at
which $v$ crosses 0.02 with all other criteria already satisfied. Every
accepted solve completes the 4,000-update Adam phase and stops during
L-BFGS, since each run records more than 4,000 loss/gradient evaluations.

Stop checks run every 100 Adam updates and after each L-BFGS block of at
most 20 iterations and nominally 25 evaluations. The measured time thus
overshoots the first true crossing by at most one check interval. For GL8,
one nominal block is at most about 0.4\% of its 6,261--9,032 recorded
evaluations. The same rule applies to every arm, so check granularity
enters paired ratios only through differing optimization trajectories.

\section{Three-dimensional transient extension: C36}\label{sec:c36}
\subsection{Scientific problem and derivative contract}
The mathematical source is OpenAI Math376's incompressible box-transport
construction~\cite{math376}. We instantiate one compactly supported box
motion in the unit cube over $t\in[0,1]$. For prescribed smooth centers
$c(t)$, a determinant-one diagonal dilation $D(t)$ and a smooth cutoff
$\chi$, the construction gives
\begin{align}
y&=x-c(t),\qquad L=\dot D D^{-1},\qquad \operatorname{tr}L=0,\\
B&=\tfrac12\dot c\times y+\tfrac13(Ly)\times y,\\
U&=\nabla\times(\chi B)=\chi(\dot c+Ly)+\nabla\chi\times B,\\
p&=0,\qquad f=\partial_t U+(U\cdot\nabla)U-\nu\Delta U.
\label{eq:math376source}
\end{align}
The prescribed motion determines the fixed external force and its spatial
derivative; both are independent of learned parameters. The cutoff curl
term is retained at box faces, edges and corners. Numerical choices belong
to this project's single-box instance, which is a constructed forced-flow
problem under stated conditions rather than a general formula for arbitrary
Navier--Stokes data or a fluid regularity theorem.

The model uses viscosity $\nu=0.01$, cutoff width $\eta=1/32$, and the
affine/$\tanh$ network widths 4--128--128--128--128--4 (50,692 parameters).
Its outputs represent a three-component curl potential and pressure, with
scales 0.01 and 0.1 respectively. The prescribed moving-box geometry
supplies input features and a compact gate; the model and particle
right-hand side use the learned potential, without inserting the exact
interior velocity. The curl construction enforces divergence freedom in
real arithmetic; numerical divergence is still measured.

For a scalar potential intermediate, the retained raw derivative state is
\begin{equation}
S_{36}(z)=(z,g,H,T,Q,m,n,K),\qquad
Q_{ij}=\partial_{ij}\Delta z,
\label{eq:c36state}
\end{equation}
where $g$, $H$ and $T$ are the spatial gradient, symmetric Hessian and
symmetric third derivative; $m=\partial_t z$, $n=\nabla\partial_t z$ and
$K=\nabla^2\partial_t z$. These blocks have
$1+3+6+10+6+1+3+6=36$ independent channels. They supply the curl velocity,
transient momentum residual and its gradient, including contracted fourth
spatial derivatives of the potential. For comparison, a complete
fourth-order jet in the four variables $(x_1,x_2,x_3,t)$ has
$\binom{8}{4}=70$ coefficients. C36 is a partial-jet contract for this
consumer, not a minimality result.

The canonical objective contains 4,096 interior, 512 initial, 384 boundary
and seven pressure-gauge points. All 4,999 points and all 50,692 parameters
are included. Frozen physical normalization and the original global
family denominators are retained, including during chunk accumulation.
FP64 and disabled TF32 are fixed. This campaign's two stored
initializations are 361901 and 361902; they are not two-dimensional formal
seeds. Source and calibration hashes and the complete protocol are part of
the artifact~\cite{flashns3dartifact}.

\subsection{Execution variants and numerical qualification}
Three successive execution variants share the C36 algebra. (i) A
packed-gather revision transposes symmetric unpacking through four cached
integer gathers, keeping dense 9- and 27-entry activation storage and
local state-autograd nonlinear pullbacks. (ii) A packed activation computes
the six Hessian and ten third-derivative entries directly, and a manual
analytic transpose reverses the activation's packed contractions and the
actual stable coefficient program. (iii) The same variant with the packed
forward compiled in both timing arms and the candidate's activation
transpose compiled. Gate and curl nonlinear pullbacks still use local state
autograd in all variants; this is not a fully manual adjoint for the
entire model. For $y=\tanh z$ and $e=\exp(-2|z|)$, the frozen coefficient
convention is
\begin{align}
a_1&=4e/(1+e)^2,\qquad a_2=-2ya_1,\\
a_3&=4y^2a_1-2a_1^2,\qquad
a_4=16ya_1^2-8y^3a_1.
\label{eq:stablec36}
\end{align}
The transpose differentiates these evaluated operations, including the
rounded $\tanh$ derivative and the exponential tail, rather than
substituting an idealized fifth derivative at saturated values. Regression
checks include values 0, $\pm10^{-16}$, $\pm10^{-12}$, $\pm20$ and
$\pm40$, with cotangents scaled to $10^8$.

The reference is a fresh native coordinate-AD evaluation on H100 at all
4,999 canonical points, for both initializations and all 50,692 parameters
(52 comparisons, 140 saved arrays, maximum error $2.07227\times10^{-5}$ of
the unchanged $10^{-11}+10^{-9}|g_{\rm ref}|$ budget). Later variants are
checked against that preserved reference at original and changed
parameters, in direct calls and graph replay, and against parameter AD.
Variant (i) passes 48 comparisons. Variants (ii) and (iii) each pass 78
comparisons over 156 arrays with maximum error $2.35186\times10^{-6}$ of
the budget; both remote and local NumPy-only readers pass every comparison.
A full canonical CPU qualification of the compiled analytic variant passes
42 comparisons (maximum $9.17612\times10^{-6}$ of the budget). Exact initial
tensor bytes come from the original checkpoints: regeneration under a newer
Torch version was rejected after 1,804 of 50,692 entries differed by one
ULP. No tolerance or scientific data was changed.

\subsection{Diagnosis of the failed acceptance}
Four observations indicate that the failure is limited by optimization
rather than by derivative execution. First, the two engines reach nearly
identical final losses from the same initialization (5.402/5.387 and
5.168/5.153 $\times10^{-4}$), and all scheduled full-gradient checks pass.
Second, the losses lie about five times above the $10^{-4}$ gate, not orders
of magnitude away. Third, the budget is small relative to the problem: each
C36 solve spends 438--449 L-BFGS evaluations on 50,692 parameters, whereas
the accepted two-dimensional solves spend 2,208--5,259 L-BFGS evaluations
after the same 4,000-update Adam phase on 4,547 parameters. Fourth, the
stratified corrected-pressure error is 6.4--8.7 times the uniform-volume
value, but this ratio mostly reflects how the two holdouts weight space:
4,033 of the 4,096 uniform-volume points lie outside the box support, where
the reference flow vanishes, whereas the stratified holdout places 90\% of
its mass inside the support. The published observations of this protocol
contain no regional breakdown; for the development run in
Section~\ref{sec:c36v2} the largest pressure error lies in the box plateau
rather than in the cutoff layers. These observations motivate a separately
preregistered protocol with a larger L-BFGS budget; they do not license
changing the completed protocol's budget, gates or seeds after observing
its outcome. The Ethier--Steinman solves in Section~\ref{sec:ethier}, whose
development run failed only its pressure gate at 100 L-BFGS blocks and
passed once the block budget alone was raised, are consistent with this
reading, although they use a different contract and problem.

\subsection{Development run with a larger L-BFGS budget}\label{sec:c36v2}
A separately frozen development protocol changes only the L-BFGS budget,
from 20 to 800 calls (closure cap 520 to 20,800). Data, model,
normalization, Adam schedule, L-BFGS settings, seeds, training limits and
every physical gate are those of the original protocol. Because seeds
361901 and 361902 were already observed, this is a reachability study
rather than a formal time-to-accuracy comparison. Both engines train both
seeds on one four-GPU H100 node: variant (iii) with its analytic activation
transpose, and parameter AD over the same compiled packed forward, each with
CUDA Graph replay. All complete loss/gradient checks pass at 28 check
points per run, against dense parameter AD and, at the original parameters,
against the preserved native reference, with maximum error
$1.16\times10^{-5}$ of the original budget. A decision rule was registered
before the run, with the conditions (a) ``Loss stays above 1e-4'', (b)
``Loss passes 1e-4 but physical errors stop falling'' and (c) ``Physical
gates pass''; branch (c) would lead to a formal protocol with new seeds and
both engines.

\subsection{Physical errors along the training trajectory}\label{sec:c36trend}
Four further run-once batches observe every saved checkpoint of the
development runs, from the untrained parameters through Adam updates
1,000--4,000 and L-BFGS calls 20--800 (52 saved points, 46 distinct
parameter vectors), and 26 checkpoints of the continuation runs of
Section~\ref{sec:c36v3}. Each of the 72 observations uses the unchanged
observer on every data plane: the stratified and uniform holdouts, seven time
slices, the initial and boundary sets, and both trajectory refinements.
Physical acceptance gates the two holdouts, the initial and boundary values
and the finer trajectory refinement; the time slices are diagnostic planes
evaluated with the same field limits. The independent NumPy reader re-reads
every observation (117 saved-array checks each, 8,424 in total) and passes.
Re-observing the two analytic development finals reproduces every metric of
their earlier observation exactly.

Each batch chooses its observer chunk by a rule written before its
calibration: the fastest chunk whose states agree with chunk 128 to
$10^{-10}$ of their maximum magnitude within a peak-memory limit. On the 512
calibration points, chunk 512 is 3.9$\times$ faster than chunk 128, agrees
to at most $1.8\times10^{-11}$ absolute ($7.9\times10^{-12}$ relative) and
peaks at 56.6\,GB; chunks 1,024 and 2,048 exceed device memory. The first
batch's 50\,GB limit, set before this measurement, kept chunk 128 (about 100
minutes per observation), so its deadline guard stopped it after 8 of 46
planned observations. Later batches observed the remaining checkpoints
with a corrected 60\,GB limit and chunk 512 (median 31 minutes). Chunk
equivalence rests on the calibration points; no full checkpoint was observed
at both chunks.

at stratified pressures of 2.49 and $2.08\times10^{-3}$ (seed 361901) and
2.49 and $3.28\times10^{-3}$ (seed 361902), which sets the scale of
trajectory sensitivity at equal budget.

\subsection{Temporal-smoothness continuation}\label{sec:c36v3}
The development runs suggest that the motion window is the hard part of the
problem. A continuation schedule therefore starts from a slower, smoother
motion and returns to the original problem. The box motion is driven by one
schedule $s(t)=\sigma(2t-\tfrac12)$, where $\sigma$ is the smooth unit
switch of the construction, so the box moves for $t\in(\tfrac14,\tfrac34)$.
A transition width $w$ generalizes it to
\begin{equation}
s_w(t)=\sigma\!\left(\frac{t}{w}-\frac{1}{2w}+\frac12\right),\qquad
\text{moving for } t\in\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac w2,\tfrac12+\tfrac w2\right),
\label{eq:smoothness}
\end{equation}
with the same displacement and dilation, centered at $t=\tfrac12$, and with
$k$-th time derivatives scaling as $w^{-k}$; $w=\tfrac12$ is the original
problem. Because the model gate contains the schedule speed, a width changes
the geometry features, the gate and the forcing; value targets remain zero,
which is checked. The original problem is reproduced bit for bit at
$w=\tfrac12$. At $w=\tfrac34$ both engines agree with native coordinate AD on
the changed objective (six CPU tests). During training each stage is entered
with a fresh compiled engine and CUDA Graph. It is checked against dense
parameter AD at the entry parameters, after a parameter change, and again
after garbage collection and allocator churn. Physical acceptance always uses
the original problem.

Two continuation schedules spend the development budget (4,000 Adam updates
and 800 L-BFGS calls with the same settings) differently. C1 runs 1,000 Adam
updates at $w=1$, 1,000 at $w=\tfrac34$, then 2,000 Adam updates and all 800
L-BFGS calls at $w=\tfrac12$. C2 runs 2,000 Adam updates at $w=1$, 2,000 Adam
updates and 100 L-BFGS calls at $w=\tfrac34$, then 700 L-BFGS calls at
$w=\tfrac12$. Each uses the same two initializations and is paired with the
analytic development run of the same seed, the $w=\tfrac12$ baseline. Loss
thresholds count only evaluations of the original problem, while evaluations
and seconds are counted from the start. The rule registered before the run
reads: helps, ``both schedules or one schedule reach every threshold sooner
and the final physical errors (stratified pressure in particular) are lower
than the baseline on both seeds''; no effect, ``differences within the
seed-to-seed spread of the baseline''; hurts, ``thresholds reached later or
final errors higher on both seeds''.

The first attempt failed in all four lanes at its first in-training check,
after 100 Adam updates. The graph-capture helper retains the graph and its
outputs, but not the engine that owns the prepared data, and the new trainer
had kept that engine only in a stage-local variable. Graph replays after
stage entry therefore read released memory, and the loss became negative from
the second update on. The batch archived itself and is preserved. The repair
keeps the engine with its replay and adds the post-churn check and a
non-negative-loss guard. It also gives the CPU self-test a replay that holds
only a weak reference to the engine, so the same error now fails the
self-test (verified by a negative control). The second attempt changes only
the trainer.

motion remains the binding gate.

\subsection{A larger L-BFGS budget}\label{sec:c36v5}
Section~\ref{sec:c36trend} leaves one direct question: does more optimizer
budget reach the stratified pressure gate? Two further studies were
registered to answer it.

\paragraph{A cancelled resumed design.}
The first design (protocol v4) resumed the final checkpoints of the C2 and
baseline runs and added 4,000 L-BFGS calls with a fresh L-BFGS history. Its
trainer verified each start checkpoint's hashes and refused a tampered
start. Every qualification check passed, and each source run's final loss
was reproduced bit for bit or within $4.2\times10^{-11}$ of the original
budget.

After 13.5 minutes, before any physical observation, the batch was
cancelled on optimizer progress alone. A fresh history started at a loss of
about $10^{-6}$ falls into a cycle: each trial step $t=1$ raises the loss to
1.3--1.9$\times10^{-6}$, and the strong-Wolfe search then accepts a tiny
step. Over 100 calls the loss fell by 0.2--0.7\%, against about 32\% over
the last 100 calls of the source runs. Fresh histories started earlier at
losses of $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-3}$ had behaved normally. The resumed design
would therefore have measured the restart, not the budget; its partial
outputs are archived. The source runs also showed that the continuation runs
had already stalled at 1.1--1.4$\times10^{-6}$ by call 800 (0.02--0.46\% per
10 calls), while the baseline runs were still falling by 3.4--3.7\% per 10
calls. The smoothness arm was therefore dropped.

\paragraph{Protocol v5.}
The second design keeps the development recipe unchanged, trainer and driver
included, and extends the L-BFGS budget from 800 to 4,800 calls in one
continuous run per seed and engine, so the L-BFGS history never restarts.
Both engines train both seeds on one four-GPU H100 node. A rule registered
before the run, implemented as code and tested on synthetic outcomes,
classifies the result from the physical observations at 1,600, 2,400, 3,200
and 4,000 calls and at the end:
\begin{itemize}
\item E1: all four runs pass;
\item E2: exactly one engine passes on both seeds;
\item E3: some runs pass;
\item E4: no run passes and the errors still fall;
\item E5: no run passes and the errors have stopped falling, meaning a final
  stratified pressure at least 0.95 times its value at 4,000 calls on both
  seeds of an engine.
\end{itemize}
E1 or E2 would lead to a formal fresh-seed time-to-accuracy protocol. The
runs do not reproduce the development runs bit for bit: the first
difference appears in the last digit of the loss between Adam updates 3 and
8, a reduction-order effect, so call 800 was observed anew.

\section{Accepted three-dimensional solves: Ethier--Steinman}\label{sec:ethier}
\subsection{Benchmark, contract and baselines}
The C36 study qualifies derivatives and throughput but yields no accepted
solve. We therefore also train on the transient three-dimensional
Ethier--Steinman flow~\cite{ethier}, which NSFnets also uses as a PINN
benchmark~\cite{nsfnets}. With $a=0.5$, $d=1$ and $\nu=1$,
\begin{equation}
u_*=-a\left[e^{ax}\sin(ay+dz)+e^{az}\cos(ax+dy)\right]e^{-\nu d^2t},
\end{equation}
$v_*$ and $w_*$ follow by cyclic permutation of $(x,y,z)$, the force is
zero, and the pressure of this Beltrami flow is $p_*=-|u_*|^2/2$ up to a
function of time. We use the cube $[-0.5,0.5]^3$, $t\in[0,0.25]$ and the
gauge $p(x,t)-p(0,0,0,t)$. At four points, evaluated at 80 and 160 decimal
digits, this field satisfies all 16 momentum, divergence and
residual-gradient observations to below $10^{-65}$.

A 4--64--64--4 $\tanh$ network with 4,740 parameters predicts
$(u,v,w,p)$ directly. Let $r$ collect the three momentum residuals and the
divergence. The FP64 objective is
\begin{equation}
L=\operatorname{mean}_{\Omega}\left(\|r\|_2^2+0.1\|\nabla_x r\|_F^2\right)
+10\operatorname{mean}_{\partial\Omega}\|u-u_*\|_2^2
+10\operatorname{mean}_{t=0}\|u-u_*\|_2^2
+\operatorname{mean}_{\rm gauge}p^2
\end{equation}
over 512 interior, 384 boundary, 256 initial and 64 gauge points, where
$\nabla_x$ is the spatial gradient. The residual reads $\partial_t u$,
$\nabla u$, $\Delta u$ and $\nabla p$; its spatial gradient additionally
reads $\nabla\partial_t u$, the full spatial Hessian (through convection and
the divergence gradient) and $\nabla\Delta u$. The C17 contract keeps
exactly these: value, spatial gradient, packed spatial Hessian, gradient of
the spatial Laplacian, $\partial_t z$ and $\nabla\partial_t z$, that is
$1+3+6+3+1+3=17$ channels. Through an activation the spatial blocks follow
Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure}, $\partial_t y=a_1\partial_t z$ and
$\nabla\partial_t y=a_1\nabla\partial_t z+a_2\,\partial_t z\,\nabla z$; diffusion
excludes time. Its explicit parameter transpose again uses $a_4$.

Two internal baselines share the data, initial parameter bytes, objective,
optimizer program, FP64 CUDA Graph replay and gates. \textbf{A24} propagates
the complete spatial third-order jet plus $\partial_t z$ and
$\nabla\partial_t z$ (24 channels) and takes its parameter gradient by
autograd. \textbf{Naive Q35} propagates all 35 monomials of $(x,y,z,t)$
through degree three with a generic autograd parameter gradient. Both are
internal implementations, not public libraries.

\subsection{Protocol}
Eight gates on separate validation sets of the same sizes must hold:
normalized momentum, divergence, boundary-velocity, initial-velocity and
gauge RMS at most 0.01, normalized residual-gradient RMS at most 0.02,
velocity relative $L_2$ at most 0.02 and pressure relative $L_2$ at most
0.04. A candidate stop must be confirmed by independent scalar coordinate
AD. Adam runs 2,000 updates with learning rate $10^{-3}$; strong-Wolfe
L-BFGS then permits at most 400 blocks of at most 20 iterations, nominally
25 evaluations, history 50 and tolerances $10^{-10}$/$10^{-12}$. Stop checks
run every 200 Adam updates and every five blocks. Before training, each
process checks the complete loss and all 4,740 gradients against scalar AD
on a small point subset and, through three in-place graph replays on all
points, against the eager A24 reference at
absolute $2\times10^{-12}$ and relative $2\times10^{-11}$.

A development protocol with 100 L-BFGS blocks, run on one H20 with seed
642201, passed seven gates in all three arms and failed only pressure
relative $L_2$ (6.95\%, 6.95\% and 7.04\%). Removing each model's own
pressure offset at the origin did not change that error, and it was still
decreasing at the final block. The successor protocol changes only the
block budget, to 400; a CPU development run of C17 on the same seed then
passed every gate. That seed is excluded. The formal protocol fixes six
seeds, 642202--642207, before any formal run; four are new draws from the
original initializer, which reproduces the earlier three bit for bit.

All 18 cells run once on two NVIDIA H100 GPUs of one node with Torch
2.4.0a0 and NumPy 1.26.4, FP64 and TF32 disabled. The three arms of a
seed share a GPU, and the six seeds use the six arm orders, so every arm
occupies every position twice. Each case is a fresh process; nothing is
retried. Times are setup and graph capture plus optimization, including
stop checks and the confirming independent check; the pre-training
qualification and the archived final field evaluation are reported
separately. An independent NumPy reader rechecks every saved qualification
gradient, the final fields against a separate 35-monomial Taylor oracle,
all metrics and every acceptance decision.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Attribution of the two-dimensional gain.}
The derivative consumer offers an execution opportunity even after
replacing nested coordinate AD with Taylor-mode computation. GL8 removes
independent third-order information that the fixed diffusion-gradient
consumer never reads, while preserving lower derivatives required by
nonlinear convection. The measured effect nevertheless combines
representation, pointwise kernels, graph scheduling and parameter backward.
GL8 removes 20\% of hidden channels, and a single 2,048-point native profile
attributes 82.30\% of the kernel-time sum to library GEMMs, yet replay is
2.06$\times$ faster than compiled Q10 (1.583 versus 3.267 ms). Most of that
replay gain is therefore likely to come from native pointwise kernels and
scheduling relative to Inductor-generated code rather than from the
channel reduction alone. The native-versus-compiled-Q10 comparison does not
isolate a pure representation effect; a Q10 arm in the same native kernels
and a GL8 arm compiled by Inductor are needed for that separation, together
with a per-kernel profile split. The problem is small (4,547 parameters,
2,048 interior points), where launch overhead is a large share of replay;
scaling in point count, width and depth is not yet measured.

\paragraph{Replay versus solve.}
Replay and solve ratios need not match. Optimizer work, line searches and
fixed validation remain outside the accelerated derivative program. Small
floating-point differences can change an L-BFGS trajectory and its closure
count. Setup also differs between compiled public adapters, JAX and explicit
native kernels, even with warmed dependency caches. We therefore report
every timing scope and actual evaluation count rather than extrapolating a
replay factor to the whole training run.

\paragraph{Chunking.}
Chunking addresses a different engineering constraint. It bounds the
intermediate execution workspace and permits a substantial allocator
reduction at the measured larger shape, while paying for input copies,
extra replay launches and gradient accumulation. The fixed data and
optimizer remain resident. We measured neither an OOM boundary nor the
maximum trainable point count, and Torch allocator counters cannot rank
total process memory against JAX's allocator.

\paragraph{Scope of the external comparisons.}
ED and collapsed Taylor supply public second-order operators extended to a
third-order gradient-enhanced consumer; this is not their original training
benchmark. Exact two-coordinate STDE preserves a deterministic target but
does not test stochastic amortization in high dimensions. The unmodified
cuEquivariance polynomial arm has an all-points Q10 boundary policy, unlike
the compact interior/value-only policy of the paper adapters. The six
initializations provide a paired view of one fixed-data manufactured PDE,
not variation across PDEs, sampled datasets, network families or hardware.
No speed claim is extended to stochastic STDE++ constructions, variable
viscosity, nonlinear coordinate maps, sample-coupled layers, second
parameter derivatives, nonlocal losses or reduced precision; all
measurements use FP64.

\paragraph{Three dimensions.}
The Ethier--Steinman solves establish an accepted three-dimensional
solve-level gain for a 17-channel transient contract, but only against
internal baselines and on a small network. The C36 study establishes
derivative correctness for the full objective of a larger curl-potential
model and a throughput gain for its gradient against an internal control,
but no accepted solve. With 800 L-BFGS calls its training passes the loss
gate, the uniform holdout and the initial and boundary tests, while
stratified pressure stays 2--3 times above its limit inside the moving box
and the trajectory endpoint is borderline. Seventy-two observations along
the training trajectories show that error still falling at that budget,
more slowly than the loss. With six times the L-BFGS budget the loss and the
errors stall, so the gap is not a budget limit of this optimizer. A
temporal-smoothness continuation changes where training meets the loss gate
but not which test fails. The C36 throughput gains depend on compilation, the zero-gate
constant folding in the artifact is qualified on CPU only, and the problem
is a single constructed box instance. External three-dimensional baselines,
accepted C36 solves, a second nonlinear PDE, reduced-precision measurements
and device-traffic measurements remain open experiments rather than implied
consequences of the implementation.

\section{Conclusion}
FlashNS gives a concrete consumer contract for high-order PINN training:
propagate a closed value/gradient/Hessian/gradient-Laplacian state and
transpose every local dependency, including the fourth activation
derivative, into the complete parameter gradient. Under an unchanged FP64
Navier--Stokes objective with independent acceptance, all 72 planned
two-dimensional H20 solves pass and GL8 shortens the paired solve time by
1.66$\times$ over a compiled full-jet control and 1.73--3.37$\times$ over
matched public derivative methods; a chunked variant trades replay time
for an 85\% lower allocated setup peak at 16,384 points. On the transient
three-dimensional Ethier--Steinman benchmark, a 17-channel contract passes
all gates in 18 of 18 H100 solves and is 3.60$\times$ and 6.96$\times$
faster than internal full spatial-jet and naive baselines. The C36 curl-potential
extension passes complete gradient qualification and improves
objective-gradient throughput by 1.32$\times$ over an equally compiled
parameter-AD control. Its original training budget does not reach the
training gate; a development run with 40 times more L-BFGS calls does, and
passes most physical gates, but stratified pressure still fails.
Observations of every checkpoint show this error still falling at that
budget. With six times the L-BFGS calls it stalls at 2.2--3.0 times its
limit, and a smoother-motion continuation does not remove it either. Accepted
C36 solves and external three-dimensional comparisons remain open.

\section{Explicit local transpose}\label{app:transpose}
For completeness, let $\bar y$, $\bar g_y$, $B_y$ and $\bar t_y$ denote
output adjoints. Here $B_y$ is symmetric and represents the Frobenius
pairing with the full symmetric Hessian. If the stored off-diagonal
Hessian channel has adjoint $\beta_{12}$, this convention sets
$(B_y)_{12}=(B_y)_{21}=\beta_{12}/2$. Define $h=\operatorname{tr}H$,
$c=g^\top g$ and $Q=gh+2Hg$. Transposing the local differential gives
\begin{align}
\bar t&=a_1\bar t_y,\\
\bar H&=a_1 B_y+a_2\big((\bar t_y^\top g)I+
                 \bar t_y g^\top+g\bar t_y^\top\big),\\
\bar g&=a_1\bar g_y+2a_2B_y g
          +a_2(h\bar t_y+2H\bar t_y)
          +a_3(c\bar t_y+2g(g^\top\bar t_y)),\\
\bar z&=a_1\bar y+a_2\bar g_y^\top g
          +\langle B_y,a_2H+a_3gg^\top\rangle_F
          +\bar t_y^\top(a_2t+a_3Q+a_4gc).
\end{align}
The packed input Hessian adjoint is obtained by transposing symmetric
unpacking: diagonal entries are unchanged and each off-diagonal channel
receives the sum of its two matrix entries. This convention prevents a
factor-of-two error. All terms contribute to the shared parameter VJP
through the affine transpose; no activation derivative order is discarded
because it exceeds the forward coordinate order.

The argument assumes fixed physical coordinates, coordinate-independent
affine parameters, a fixed contraction and sufficiently smooth pointwise
activations. Let $P$ map a complete third-order raw jet to $(z,g,H,t)$.
The chain rule establishes $P F_{\rm full}=F_{\rm GL8}P$ for each supported
layer. Induction gives the same identity for a supported network. Since
the residual loss factors through $P$, its real-arithmetic parameter
derivative agrees with the full-jet derivative. This is a closure and
transpose argument for a stated consumer; it is not a proof that no other
representation could use fewer channels.

\section{Chunk accumulation and storage contract}\label{app:chunk}
Partition interior and boundary points into finite chunks. With the
original weights, the loss is the sum of chunk contributions and its
parameter gradient is the sum of their parameter gradients. The executor
zeros accumulators once at the beginning of a closure, copies a chunk into
the reusable graph inputs, replays, and accumulates its loss and gradient.
Tail padding has zero weight. Every chunk uses the same parameter version;
the optimizer can update parameters only after the complete closure.

For maximum chunk size $M$, intermediate derivative storage is proportional
to $M$ times the retained layer-channel widths. Fixed point data still grow
with total point count, and parameter/optimizer storage is not bounded by
$M$. The eight-channel contraction removes two hidden coordinate channels;
it does not by itself prove a 20\% reduction in total live memory. At 2,048
interior points, compact logical point/channel rows decrease from
$10(2048)+512=20,992$ to $8(2048)+512=16,896$. Actual allocator measurements
include further temporaries and graph pools.

Captured outputs are borrowed until the next replay. Graph parameter
storage can be updated in place; replacing its storage or using an
incompatible stream/layout is rejected. Comparing a previously returned
borrowed scalar after another closure would compare an overwritten value.
The validation harness checks each live loss/gradient before replay and
checks updated parameter storage separately.
